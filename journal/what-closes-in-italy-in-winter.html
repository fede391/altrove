What closes in Italy in winter, region by region

Large parts of the Amalfi Coast are shut from November to March, and almost nobody selling you a winter trip wants to put that in writing. So here it is in writing.

Vincenzo Granato & Federico Capellini  ·  Altrove, Destination Management in Italy  ·  Updated September 2026

Every year we get the same email around this time. Someone has found a beautiful hotel in Positano, the rate in February is a third of what it was in July, and they want to know whether they have found a bargain or a trap.

Usually it is a trap, and not because of the hotel.

You can book a room on the Amalfi Coast in February. The room will be open. The question nobody answers is whether anything around it will be: the restaurant you wanted, the boat to Capri, the shop, the beach club, the path down to the water. On that coast, between November and March, the honest answer is often no.

The country does not shut down in the off season. Particular places do, and they are not the ones people expect.

This is not an argument against coming to Italy in winter. We spend a good part of our year building trips here between October and April, and we think it is the best time to come. It is an argument against coming to the wrong part of Italy in winter, which is a different mistake entirely and an easy one to avoid once somebody tells you the truth about it.

The short version

WhereNovember to March
Amalfi Coast, Capri, IschiaLargely closed. Many hotels, restaurants and boat services stop, ferry timetables thin out sharply, and Positano in particular empties. Sorrento and Salerno keep functioning.
Lake Como, Garda, MaggiorePartly closed. A number of the better hotels and lakeside restaurants shut from roughly November to March, and boat services run a reduced winter timetable. The towns themselves stay alive.
Cinque TerreOpen but quiet. Trails and trains keep running, some hotels and restaurants close, and the boat service between the villages is seasonal.
Puglia coast, Sardinia, smaller islandsLargely closed outside the main towns. Seaside masserie and resorts are seasonal businesses.
DolomitesOpen from December to early April for the winter season. November and the second half of April are the genuine dead weeks: lifts shut, many hotels closed.
SicilyOpen all year. Mid-teens Celsius on the coast in January, blood orange season, baroque towns with nobody in them.
Umbria, Le Marche, AbruzzoOpen all year. These are working regions, not seasonal ones.
PiedmontOpen, and October to December is its moment: white truffle, Barolo, the vineyards after the harvest.
TuscanyOpen. The cities all year; some rural agriturismi close in January and February.
Rome, Florence, Naples, Bologna, VeniceOpen all year, and considerably better without the summer crowds.

Why the coast closes and the cities do not

It comes down to who lives there. A restaurant in Trastevere serves Romans in February. A restaurant in Positano serves people who are not there in February, so the family that runs it closes for four months, repaints, and goes to see relatives. That is a rational decision and it is not going to change.

Which means the seasonality of Italy is not really about weather. It is about whether a place has a resident population large enough to keep it running once the visitors stop coming. Use that as your filter and you can predict most of the table above without looking anything up.

Where we send people instead

Sicily in January

Coastal Sicily in January sits in the mid-teens Celsius, high fifties Fahrenheit. It is citrus season. Noto, Ragusa and Modica are baroque towns built to be looked at, and there is nobody standing in front of them. The markets in Palermo do not have an off season.

Umbria and Le Marche, all winter

These regions never turned themselves into tourist infrastructure in the first place, so there is nothing to switch off. Norcia, Spoleto, the hill towns, and a food culture that is at its best in the cold months.

Piedmont, October to December

Truffle season, and the tail of the harvest. Alba in November is one of the few places in Italy that is genuinely busier in autumn than in August, and it earns it.

The Dolomites, December to early April

Very much open, if you get the dates right. The mistake is booking November, or the week after Easter when Easter falls late. Between those two windows the mountains are extraordinary and the valleys are running at full strength.

The one thing to check before you book anything

When you find a hotel with a winter rate that looks too good, do not write to the hotel. Write to two restaurants nearby and ask whether they will be open on your dates. Hotels answer that question optimistically. Restaurants answer it accurately, because they are the ones who have already decided.

If both reply that they are closed until Easter, you have your answer, and you have it for the price of two emails.
