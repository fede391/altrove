How to find the Italian town your family came from

Almost everything you need is already written down somewhere, and most of it is free. The hard part is not the research. It is what to do when you are actually standing in the piazza.

Vincenzo Granato & Federico Capellini  ·  Altrove, Destination Management in Italy  ·  Updated September 2026

There are something like eighty million people of Italian descent living outside Italy, seventeen to twenty million of them in the United States. A large number of them know the country their family came from and not the town. They have a surname, a region if they are lucky, and a story about a great-grandmother that has been retold enough times that nobody is sure which parts are true.

This is how you turn that into an address. We do this work for the travelers we take to their ancestral villages, but none of the first steps require us, or anyone else you have to pay. Do them yourself. It is genuinely one of the more satisfying things you can do with a winter evening.

Step one: the manifest, not the family story

Start with the ship. Passenger manifests from the great migration years are detailed documents, and from roughly 1906 onward they typically record the last place of residence and often the town of birth, alongside the name of a relative left behind and the name and address of whoever the passenger was going to in America.

That single line usually contains everything you have been missing. Search the Ellis Island records through the Statue of Liberty – Ellis Island Foundation site, or through FamilySearch, which is free and requires only an account. If your family arrived through Boston, Philadelphia, New Orleans or Baltimore, the equivalent lists exist too.

If the manifest gives you nothing, try naturalization papers. A Declaration of Intention or a Petition for Naturalization from the twentieth century will normally state a place of birth, sometimes down to the town.

The Ellis Island name change did not happen

It is worth clearing this up, because it stops a lot of people before they start. Inspectors at Ellis Island did not rename anybody. The passenger lists were written at the port of departure, in Naples or Palermo or Genoa, by clerks who spoke Italian, and the inspectors in New York worked from those existing lists. Surnames changed afterwards, over years, usually because families simplified them themselves to get along.

Which is good news: the spelling on the manifest is probably the real one. Search for the Italian version, not the American one.

Step two: the records are online, and they are free

Once you have a town, you can usually go back four or five generations from your sofa.

WhereWhat you will find
Portale Antenati
antenati.cultura.gov.it		The Italian state archives' own digitisation project. Scanned civil registers of births, marriages and deaths, comune by comune. Free, no account needed. The interface is in Italian but the registers are images, so the language barrier is smaller than it looks.
FamilySearchDecades of microfilmed Italian civil records, now largely digitised and indexed. Free with an account. Often easier to search than Antenati, and the two collections do not overlap perfectly, so check both.
The comune itselfThe municipal registry office holds the originals and can issue certificates, including recent ones that are not online for privacy reasons.
The parishFor anything before civil registration: baptisms, marriages and burials, many running back to the late 1500s.

How far back the civil records go depends on where your family was from, and the answer surprises people:

  • The south — Campania, Puglia, Basilicata, Calabria, Abruzzo, Molise: civil registration begins around 1809, under Napoleonic rule. Sicily follows in 1820.
  • The rest of Italy: 1866, after unification.

So if your family came from a village in Calabria or Sicily, which statistically is likely, you have two full centuries of state records waiting, written in a formulaic Italian that repeats the same phrases on every page. You will learn to read it in an afternoon.

Step three: writing to the comune

At some point you will want a document the archives do not have, or confirmation that a family still exists in the town. Write to the comune. Small Italian municipalities answer email, sometimes slowly, and they answer Italian faster than English.

Gentile Ufficio di Stato Civile,

sono un cittadino statunitense e sto ricostruendo la storia della mia famiglia. Il mio bisnonno, [NOME COGNOME], risulta nato a [PAESE] il [DATA].

Sarei molto grato se poteste confermarmi l'esistenza dell'atto di nascita e indicarmi come richiederne copia.

Vi ringrazio molto per la cortesia e la disponibilità.

Cordiali saluti,
[NOME]

In English: I am a US citizen researching my family history. My great-grandfather, name, appears to have been born in town on date. I would be grateful if you could confirm the birth record exists and tell me how to request a copy.

Send it to the address on the comune's own website, which will be something like anagrafe@comune.[nome].[sigla].it. Be patient, and be polite: you are asking a very small office to do you a favour.

What nobody tells you about the visit

This is the part we care most about, because it is where these trips go wrong.

You will find the town. You will drive up, and it will be a hillside village of nine hundred people, and there will be nothing to do there. No museum about your family. No plaque. The church will be locked. Two old men outside the bar will look at your rental car with mild interest. If you have flown four thousand miles for a moment of recognition, that hour can be genuinely deflating, and we have watched it happen.

What changes it is arriving as an expected guest rather than as a tourist.

That means someone has called the parish priest in advance, so the church is open and the registers are on the table. It means the comune knows you are coming and has looked things up. It means a local researcher has been through the records the week before, so you are reading a family tree rather than starting one in a corridor. And where there are still relatives in the town, and there very often are, it means somebody has approached them first, carefully, so that a meeting is a welcome rather than an ambush.

The village does not need to be curated. It needs to be expecting you.

Handled that way, the same nine hundred people and the same locked church become the strongest day of the trip, and usually the one the family talks about for years afterwards.

Two honest warnings

Citizenship is a separate question, and the rules changed

Many people begin this research hoping it leads to an Italian passport. The law on citizenship by descent was tightened substantially in 2025 and the generational limits are considerably narrower than they were before. We do not handle citizenship applications and we will not advise you on them. If that is your goal, speak to a lawyer who specialises in it before you spend money gathering documents.

Not every story survives contact with the records

Sometimes the village in the family legend is not the village in the register. Sometimes the trade everyone was proud of is written down as contadino, farm labourer, which is what most of them were. In our experience the real version is better, because it is actually theirs. But it is worth being ready for it.
